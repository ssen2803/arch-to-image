From excavation report to site image, as far as the evidence goes

One sentence: take a written excavation report and produce an image of the site that goes as far as the evidence in the text, and no further. The left half is a plan drawn from the layout of an example report. The right half is what Stable Diffusion draws when the same layout guides it. Both show the same two rooms, so you can drag the handle and compare them. The example text is written by us in the style of real reports; it is not a real report.

Excavation destroys what it uncovers. The report is what remains.

People who need to see a site have to rebuild it in their heads from dense technical text.

WhoWhat they needHow it is done today

Researchers and studentsTo picture walls, floors, pits and the relations between themReading text beside a plan, when a plan exists
Museums and the publicAn image of the placeA commissioned artist, or an image generator that nobody checks

Why it is hard

Reports leave things out, describe layers that hide one another, and say different things with different certainty. An image has to show all of that without inventing the rest.

Evidence that the need is real

Informal feedback from Israel Antiquities Authority staff (Nov 2026, paraphrased): a tool that checks text against plans would help editors, and the text alone gives only part of what the plan shows.

Excavation is a one-time experiment: once a layer is dug it is gone, and the report is the record. Three groups need to see the site. Editors and reviewers check by eye that text, plans and sections match. Researchers read text next to a plan, if there is one. Museums pay an artist or, more and more, use an image generator. I asked the Israel Antiquities Authority for permission to use published reports. The replies were informal, so I paraphrase them and name nobody: checking text against plans would help editors, and the text gives only part of the picture. That second point shaped the research question.

This is the problem in one picture. The report describes a grain silo with ash and charred grain. Click. The model draws a swimming pool. The place and the size are right, because they came from the layout. The water is invented, and I know why: the conditioning network was trained on the ADE20K colours, and the closest class to a pit there is called swimming pool. A person looking at the picture cannot tell which part came from the evidence. So the goal is not only an image. It is an image plus a measurement of what in it is supported.

The task: text in, layout and image out, with every element traceable

Given a written excavation report (Hebrew), produce a structured spatial layout and a top-down image for archaeologists, such that every drawn element is backed by a quote from the report and unsupported content is measured.

Input

  • Free text of one report, in the style of Hadashot Arkheologiyot
  • Walls, floors, installations and pits, with sizes and relations

Output

  • JSON layout: 5 classes, positions in metres, 9 relation types, evidence level, verbatim quote
  • 512×512 image, always labelled as hypothetical
  • Scores for faithfulness and for invented content

Constraints

  • Laptop GPU with 6 GB of memory
  • No invented positions: unknown stays unknown
  • No Antiquities Authority material before written consent

Second task, part B of the course project. Input: a top-down excavation photo. Output: a label for every pixel (background, floor, wall, installation, pit). Train a small network on our generated images and compare it with zero-shot models on real drone photos.

The formal task. Input: the free text of one report, usually Hebrew. Output: a structured layout, an image made from it, and scores. Three constraints shape the design: a 6 GB laptop GPU, the rule that an unknown position stays unknown, and copyright, because the Antiquities Authority material needs written consent. Part B is a second, smaller task: segmentation of excavation photos with a network trained on the generated images.

This is the heart of the first stage. Each click highlights the exact phrase and draws the element it supports. The outer wall, the two pieces of the partition with a doorway between them. The fourth wall is drawn dotted, because the report only proposes it: the stones were robbed and the plaster floor stops in a straight line. Then the plaster floor, the bench, the earth floor and the oven. A language model writes this layout. A deterministic validator checks that every quote appears word for word and that every relation, such as "west of" or "adjoins", really holds in the geometry. If something fails, the errors go back to the model and it tries again.

{{ plan('ex07_sealed_silo', s=60, pad=34, fs=18, loc=False) }}

Exists, but buried

A silo sealed under a floor. A rule-based stratigraphy engine removes it from the image, so the language model does not decide.

This is what sets the project apart. First, a wall the report only proposes is drawn as a proposal. Second, when the report says a pit exists but not where, the system shows the zone it could be in instead of picking a point. Third, a silo sealed under a plaster floor should not be visible from above. A deterministic engine computes visibility from relations such as cuts, on and under. There is one more rule: the system uses only as specific a term as the text does. If the text says pit, it does not become refuse pit.

What is ours

  • Controlled extraction: ontology, schema, geometric validator, quote grounding, repair loop
  • Uncertainty handling: zone instead of point, hidden layers removed, no more specific than the text
  • Evaluation: three lenses, error split by stage, unsupported-content metrics calibrated on real images

What we reuse

Claude, Stable Diffusion 1.5, ControlNet-seg, SegFormer, GroundingDINO, SAM, ResNet-18.

Full: yes. Half: partial. Empty: no or not reported. From our review of 35 works, 01_literature_review/reports/coverage_matrix.csv.

The general recipe, where a language model plans a layout and diffusion draws it, is from 2023: LayoutGPT and LMD. ControlNet++ gave us the idea of measuring by segmenting the output again. A 2025 paper compares ControlNet with GLIGEN after fine-tuning on table settings. In heritage, Li and colleagues read a site plan with a language model but do not measure images, and HARF turns evidence into prompts without layout conditioning. None of them encodes uncertainty. The right side lists what is ours and what we reuse.

Four stages, one trained model, one fixed generator

1. Extract

Report text to JSON layout. Language model (Claude) plus validator and repair loop, up to 3 attempts.

2. Generate

Layout to mask to image. Stable Diffusion 1.5 + ControlNet-seg. Compared: text only, GLIGEN.

3. Measure

SegFormer re-segments the image; the result is compared with the reference layout.

4. Train and test

U-Net with a ResNet-18 encoder, 14.4M parameters, trained on generated images. Tested on real drone photos.

Why synthetic

We found no labelled set of excavation photos with floors, pits and installations.

What is controlled

Period, building or open area, number and kind of elements, sizes in metres, doorways, light, seed. Layouts pass the same validator as layouts from text.

How it is checked

Splits by layout, agreement with an outside segmenter, comparison with real photos, and a human audit sample of 30 images.

If consent is delayed: reduced version against text layouts only; the plan comparison (H2) waits. No Antiquities Authority material is in the repository.

Four sets. The main experiment needs real reports with matching plans. I asked the Antiquities Authority in writing on 14 November; the replies are positive but there is no formal consent yet. Until then I work with example texts. The generated images come from random layouts that pass the same validator, and we control period, context, element types, sizes, doorways, lighting and seed. The test photos are real: drone tiles with wall outlines under an MIT licence. Synthetic images are never used as test data for the claim about real photos.

Research question

How much does a layout that a language model extracts from an excavation report improve the spatial faithfulness of the generated image over text alone, and how close does it come to a layout taken from the original plan?

H1. A structured layout gives higher spatial faithfulness than text alone.

H2. A layout from the plan does better than one from the text. It may fail: the plan can hold details the report never mentions.

The research question, with three conditions that share everything except the spatial information. H1: structure beats text alone. H2: the plan beats the text. H2 may fail, and that would be interesting, because the plan can contain details that are not in the report. The unit of analysis is the case. I average over seeds within a case and compare conditions in pairs.

Where was information lost? The error is split into extraction, generation and final image, so a bad score points to a stage.

Who checks the checker? The automatic segmenter makes mistakes, so 171 marked regions in 30 images will be rated by a person.

Faithfulness to the report is not the same thing as matching the plan, so they are separate lenses. A pit that appears only in the plan would punish a system that is perfectly faithful to the text. HOR is the share of drawn objects that have no support in the layout. HAR is the share of area that should be empty and was filled. For segmentation the metric is mean IoU, and on the real tiles wall IoU, because only walls are labelled there. Each criterion is concrete, so we will know if we failed.

Two facts make me confident. First, memory: generation peaks at 3.1 GB and training at 1.7 GB, so everything runs on my laptop. Second, a small pilot with hand-built layouts. Object recall goes from about 0.2 with text alone to about 0.63 with ControlNet. But it has a cost that I want to show honestly: ControlNet fills empty area, it closes rooms with walls that are not in the layout, and so the mean IoU to the layout goes down. An image with no structures at all gets a low HAR and has its ground right, so these numbers have to be read together. These are pilot numbers and do not answer the research question, because the layouts were built by hand.
